# Works Like Us website brief

This is the source of truth for the initial Works Like Us website. The Business Builder website method governs how the belief system is translated into a credible visitor journey; it does not replace the belief system.

## Fixed strategic belief from Yusuf

- The future is having your own LLM trained on your own weights, with multiple LLMs for different purposes.
- If you use the frontier models, you’ll become a commodity because:
  - Intelligence is the main source of competitive advantage.
  - If you base your business processes and decisions on common knowledge in frontier LLMs that everybody can access, nothing differentiates you.
  - To keep your competitive edge, you must train your own LLMs and build processes and applications around them. That literally is the differentiator in the future. It’s similar to humans: if you use consultants for everything and let them make every decision, everything becomes the same and price competition is all that remains.
- Listen to Alex Karp’s famous interview—I think on CNBC a few months ago—where he lays out the case for this.
- What can companies do with their own LLMs?
  - We build their custom processes, agents and workflows.
  - Provide examples of how this can create competitive advantage.
- I currently have no idea what the business case is, so you’ll have to develop something—or perhaps avoid publishing prices because every project is unique.
  - What is known is that we build, deploy and maintain custom LLMs, processes and business systems run by those LLMs.

Build the website so it conveys the best version of this imaginary business as if it already exists.

The target is enterprise.

Include a section about privacy and how important it is that companies preserve it.

In general, listen to Alex Karp’s interview and read reactions on X. Find discussions about the interview, real enterprise customers talking about these issues and their needs, and other relevant influencers—for example, the founder of Box, who talks about this frequently—and present the business as though it exists.

## Website assignment

- Brand: **Works Like Us**.
- Primary audience: enterprise and B2B SME decision-makers, with Swedish companies as the first market.
- Relevant roles: managing director or operations leader, technical leader, security or legal reviewer, and the people who will use the first system.
- Primary visitor action: submit enough information for Works Like Us to propose the first company-specific AI system worth testing.
- Languages: complete English and Swedish journeys at `/en` and `/se`.
- Publication target: a public v1 on `workslikeus.com`, with `workslikeus.ai` also desired if it is still available and the exact purchase is approved.
- Scope: explain the business as a real offer without inventing customers, outcomes, certifications, model ownership, delivery capacity or commercial terms that do not yet exist.

### Reasonable inferences used for the initial website

- A buyer is more likely to engage around one costly or inconsistent workflow than around an abstract company-wide AI transformation.
- The first engagement should therefore identify one business result, build with a controlled slice of real data, and let the people doing the work decide whether the system earned expansion.
- Existing frontier models may still be useful components. The company’s durable advantage can include its own weights where justified, while also including the knowledge, decisions, workflows, evaluations, permissions and learning record built around any model.
- Buyers using HubSpot, Salesforce, Pipedrive or Microsoft 365 will usually prefer an AI system that initially works with those tools instead of requiring an immediate replacement.
- Privacy claims are credible only when the deployment boundary, providers, access, retention, regions and audit trail are decided for the actual system.

### Untested hypotheses

- Enterprise buyers will agree that relying only on generally available frontier intelligence makes their operations more interchangeable.
- Owning or training company-specific model weights will be necessary often enough to be a central commercial proposition, rather than one implementation option among several.
- “Build AI that works the way your company works” will create more qualified interest than leading with model ownership, efficiency or privacy alone.
- A four-week first-system structure is commercially and operationally realistic.
- A founder-led enquiry is the right initial conversion action.
- The initial offer, price, delivery capacity and ongoing maintenance model remain unproven.

## External evidence that shaped the initial website

Reviewed on 25 August 2026. These sources inform the website hypothesis; they are not proof that Works Like Us can deliver results or that buyers will convert.

- In Alex Karp's 1 July 2026 CNBC interview, he argued that technical customers want control of their compute, models, data stack and company-specific advantage. He also framed a useful enterprise system as a model combined with an application layer and compute, and named concrete trust questions about data ownership, caching, prompt security and provider access. His broader claims about frontier labs and enterprise anger are Palantir's commercially interested position, not independently verified facts. Sources: [CNBC Squawk Pod recording](https://www.tapesearch.com/episode/palantir-ceo-alex-karp-ceos-are-livid-7-1-26/LY3cnM9rJbiGYkmofaYWbf) and [full interview transcript](https://github.com/lhl/realitycheck-data/blob/main/reference/primary/karp-2026-cnbc-squawk-box-tokenmaxxing-transcript.md).
- Box's 2026 enterprise-AI report says the main gap is no longer model access but connecting agents to trusted company content. Its surveyed IT leaders also named security, privacy, compliance, fragmented systems, integration and permissions as material barriers. This supports the site's emphasis on company knowledge, existing systems, explicit controls and auditability. It is vendor-sponsored research and should be treated as directional evidence. Source: [Box, “Enterprise content becomes the AI bottleneck”](https://blog.box.com/SAI26-enterprise-content-becomes-the-ai-bottleneck).
- A Box/AWS discussion recommends starting with a business objective, the minimum relevant data and guardrails; it also emphasizes model choice, existing workflows and architecture that can improve over time. That supports the first-workflow offer and provider-exit language. Source: [Box, “How AWS and Box are helping enterprises turn AI ambition into agentic execution”](https://blog.box.com/how-aws-and-box-are-helping-enterprises-turn-ai-ambition-agentic-execution).
- Aaron Levie has argued on X that companies should build the software that expresses a core competency and rent the undifferentiated remainder. He has separately warned that rapidly improving frontier models can make custom scaffolding obsolete. Together these points challenge an indiscriminate “build everything yourself” pitch and support the site's narrower claim: use strong general models where helpful while owning the company-specific context, workflow, evaluations and learning—and train weights where that creates a real advantage. Sources: [Aaron Levie on core competencies](https://x.com/levie/status/2012353041782263965) and [Aaron Levie on changing agent architecture, reposted by Dharmesh Shah](https://x.com/dharmesh/status/2040085435821543459).
- Public reactions to Karp's interview split between support for sovereign, controlled AI and criticism that his claims were self-serving or too broad. The site therefore does not repeat claims that frontier providers steal customer data or that every company must immediately train a model. It converts the controversy into questions a buyer can verify for each system: providers, permissions, retention, regions, audit records, model portability and ownership.
